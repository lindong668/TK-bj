Read Before Use: 3 Common Image Processing Pitfalls

Every tool here runs locally in your browser — no upload, no account, no waiting. These notes are drawn from our original long-form guides and cover the mistakes we see most often when preparing images for a website, a storefront or a client.

New: Lab Notes — six dated, measured reports built from specimen files and real screenshots of these tools, including a favicon rendering field test, a transparency accident report and a printable 12-cell icon QA sheet. What changed and when →

Avoid QR codes and faces in small icons

16×16 gives you just 256 pixels, so a QR code, a face or a line of small type collapses into a blur. At that size use a single letter, a flat two-colour mark or a simple geometric shape; start at 32 px when you need detail, and 64 px for photos and QR codes. A proper .ico bundles 16, 32, 48, 64, 128 and 256 px entries, because browser tabs, Windows, Google results and iOS home screens each request a different one.

Read the guide: icon sizes & pixel standards →

WebP is the practical default, not a universal one

For photographs, lossy WebP is typically 25–35% smaller than JPG at comparable quality, and it supports alpha transparency too. AVIF can go smaller still, but encoding is slow and support in older CMS and email pipelines remains patchy. Keep PNG for screenshots, line art and anything with sharp edges or transparency; keep GIF for animation only, since it is capped at 256 colours.

Read the guide: choosing an image format →

Photos carry more than pixels

EXIF metadata can include GPS coordinates, capture time, camera and lens details, and the software used. Most social platforms strip it on upload, but originals emailed to a client, attached to a support ticket or dropped into a WordPress media library often keep it. Strip metadata before sharing any photo you did not take in a public place, especially shots taken at home.

Four jobs that need more than one tool

Most real tasks are a short chain rather than a single click. These are the four combinations people use most often here, with the order that actually works and the limit of each step spelled out.

🎯 1 · A favicon for a new site Upload any square-ish image, drag the crop box over the mark, tick 16 / 32 / 48 / 64 / 128 / 256 and download one multi-size .ico. Add the 180 px PNG separately for iOS home screens. A QR code or a line of small type disappears at 16 px — use a letter or a flat shape below 32 px. 🛍️ 2 · A storefront product set Trim Edges removes the uniform studio margin → Crop makes every shot square → Resize with the 800×800 preset gives identical frames → Compress brings each file under the marketplace limit. Compress last: resizing after compression re-exposes artefacts. 📱 3 · One long screenshot, then slices Stitch vertically at gap 0 to join captures taken at the same width, then Split by fixed height — 1500 px fits most chat and ticket windows. Splitting copies pixels without rescaling, so the last slice is sharp but the total weight stays roughly the same. 🖨️ 4 · Scans into a printable PDF Trim Edges → Rotate & Flip if the page is sideways → Images to PDF at A4, quality 85. Each page is capped at 2000 px, which is about 242 dpi across A4 — enough for an office printer, below a print shop's 300 dpi request. Transparency is flattened onto white.

The practical rule: choose the format before editing, not after. Re-saving a JPEG three times across different tools compounds compression damage, while PNG intermediates keep every step clean and can be converted once at the end.

FAQ

Are my images uploaded to a server?
No. Everything happens locally in your browser. Your images are never uploaded to any server, so your privacy is fully protected.
Do I need to sign up?
No. PicCalc is completely free — just open it and start. No registration needed.
Which image formats are supported?
Input accepts PNG, JPG/JPEG, WebP, GIF, BMP, SVG, AVIF, ICO and HEIC/HEIF. Output depends on the page: the editors write PNG (lossless, keeps transparency) and only Convert Format writes JPG, WebP or ICO. HEIC is input-only, because browsers decode it but cannot encode it.
Is there a usage limit?
No limits. You can use every tool as many times as you like.
Are processed images stored on your servers?
No. Results live only in the memory of your current browser tab and disappear when you close the page. Download them promptly — we keep no copies.
Do you add a watermark?
No. Images from every PicCalc tool come without any watermark.
What image sizes are supported?
We recommend under 5 MB per image with edges no longer than 3000 px. Larger images take longer to process locally and are more likely to hit memory limits. Photos shot on a phone can usually be processed directly.
Why do automatic tools struggle with some images?
Edge trimming here scans rows and columns and compares each pixel with the border colour within a tolerance you control; it works best on uniform solid borders. Gradients, drop shadows and anti-aliased edges can be misread as content — check the preview and adjust tolerance before downloading.
Does it work on mobile?
Yes. The layout adapts to mobile, and your phone browser can upload photos straight from the gallery. Large images will process noticeably slower on a phone than on a computer.
