How it works

A filter applies a fixed mathematical transform to every pixel's colour. Black & white is not a plain average of the RGB channels but a weighting by how sensitive the eye is to each colour: green contributes most to luma (about 0.587), red next (0.299) and blue least (0.114). That is what makes the greyscale match human perception.

A vintage filter typically lifts red and green while suppressing blue, and slightly lowers overall contrast, mimicking the yellowed, faded look of old film. Cool raises blue and lowers red; warm does the opposite.

Strength interpolates linearly between the original and target colour: result = original + (target − original) × strength. So 0 leaves the image untouched, 1 applies the filter fully, and values in between blend the two proportionally.

FAQ about filters

Can a filter be undone?
On the page you can reset to the original or adjust strength. But a downloaded file cannot be undone, so keep the original.
What strength looks natural?
For portraits, 30–60 usually looks natural. Above 80 the effect is visibly exaggerated — fine if you want a strong style.
Is black & white the same as dropping saturation?
Not quite. Lowering saturation keeps luminance relationships but retains colour structure, whereas true black & white conversion weights by eye sensitivity, giving different tonal separation.
Why is processing slow on large images?
Filters compute pixel by pixel, so larger images take longer. Use the Resize tool to bring the long edge under 2000 px first.
