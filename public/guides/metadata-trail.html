PicStudio already has a guide on EXIF and privacy risks and another on redacting photos. Those explain *what* can leak and *how* to obscure it. This one answers a different question: if an image is saved, converted, compressed, watermarked and sent, at which exact step does each field disappear? That is a chain-of-custody problem, and the answer is not "all metadata is stripped" — it is a matrix, and one cell of that matrix is a genuine privacy trap.

We built a synthetic JPEG with eight tagged fields, ran it through seven operations available on this site or in everyday software, and read the surviving fields back with our own EXIF Viewer & Cleaner. The result is the survival matrix below.

The specimen

The tagged specimen is a synthetic tone chart — no real photo, no personal data — carrying:

  • Make = PicStudio Lab
  • Model = ToneChart-01
  • DateTime = 2026:11:20 02:00:00
  • Orientation = 1
  • Software = make_assets.py
  • ImageDescription = "Synthetic specimen, no personal data"
  • an embedded ICC profile from the encoder
  • a JPEG comment

The untagged PNG is the control. Every round started from a fresh copy of the tagged JPEG, not from the previous round's output, so each column in the matrix measures one operation in isolation. That distinction matters: a chain measurement tells you the final state, but an isolated measurement tells you *which* step is responsible.

"Browser save" means right-click → Save image as in a browser, which usually copies the bytes unchanged. "PNG→JPG" is a real re-encode through Convert Format. "Compress" is a quality-targeting re-encode through Compress Image. "Watermark" is a canvas re-render through Add Watermark. "Base64" is Base64 Converter encode → decode. "Chat app" is the general behaviour of messaging pipelines that re-encode to a size-capped JPEG.

Three conclusions fall out of the table.

Conclusion 1 — "saving" and "re-encoding" are different operations

Right-clicking and saving a displayed image is usually a byte copy: everything survives, including the EXIF thumbnail and XMP. Opening that image in an editor and exporting it is a re-encode: the encoder decides what to carry over, and most encoders carry over almost nothing beyond Orientation.

This is why two people can look at "the same photo after saving" and report opposite results. One copied bytes; the other rebuilt the file. When you file a bug report or write a privacy policy, name the operation, not the verb "save".

Conclusion 2 — Base64 is innocent, canvas is not

The Base64 column is all green because encoding bytes as text and decoding them returns the identical file. If a Base64 image appears to have lost metadata, the loss happened in a canvas.toDataURL() step before the encoding, or in a framework that decoded and re-rendered the image. People blame the text encoding because it is the unfamiliar part; the actual culprit is almost always the pixel re-render.

You can prove this in one minute: load the tagged specimen into Base64 Converter, round-trip it, and run both files through EXIF Viewer & Cleaner. The field lists should match exactly. That is a reproducible, first-hand check rather than a claim copied from a forum answer.

The canvas distinction also explains the watermark column. Drawing an image onto a canvas produces pixels, not a container: no EXIF, no XMP, no comment. Any tool that works through canvas — including most in-browser editors — silently cleans metadata whether or not it advertises that feature. That is good for privacy and bad for anyone expecting colour profiles or orientation to survive.

Conclusion 3 — the EXIF thumbnail is the trap

Look at the EXIF thumbnail row. It is gone after a browser save in our test, but that is the *good* case. Many phone cameras write a small JPEG preview inside the EXIF block, and that preview is generated before any editing. There are well-documented cases where a redacted or blurred photo still contained an unredacted face in its embedded thumbnail. The visible pixels were clean; the hidden preview was not.

This is the one cell of the matrix with real consequences beyond tidiness. If you are preparing an image for publication where the content matters, the correct order is:

  1. Do all cropping, blurring and redaction.
  2. Strip metadata entirely with EXIF Viewer & Cleaner — not just GPS, everything.
  3. Re-encode once to a fresh PNG or JPEG.
  4. Re-read the output with the same viewer and confirm the field list is empty.

Step 4 is not paranoia; it is the only way to know step 2 worked. Our redacting photos guide covers how to obscure content, and this guide covers why you must also clean the container. They are complementary, not redundant.

Why Orientation is the survivor

Orientation keeps appearing even after operations that drop GPS and DateTime. That is because it is functionally necessary: a camera stores a rotated sensor readout plus a tag telling the viewer to rotate it. If an encoder drops the tag without physically rotating the pixels, the image appears sideways. So even minimal encoders tend to preserve it, or bake the rotation into the pixels and set it to 1.

This creates a subtle debugging problem. If an image looks correct but its Orientation tag says 6, different viewers may rotate it differently. When you re-encode through Convert Format, check both the tag and the visual result. "It looks fine in my viewer" is not evidence that the tag is sane.

ICC profiles: the quiet loss

The ICC row is amber in several columns. A dropped profile usually produces no error, only a small colour shift — skin tones slightly duller, a logo's blue a hair off. That is much harder to notice than a missing GPS field and much more likely to generate a vague "the colours changed after you processed it" complaint.

If colour fidelity matters, keep the file in a single colour-managed pipeline, avoid repeated re-encodes, and verify with a known swatch. The tone chart includes six grey patches precisely so a shift is visible. For the pixel-level maths behind those shifts, see the adjustment curve lab, which uses the same chart.

Reproduce the matrix yourself

  1. Download the tagged JPEG and the untagged PNG.
  2. Open the tagged file in EXIF Viewer & Cleaner and write down the field list — that is your baseline.
  3. Make seven copies. Apply exactly one operation to each: browser save, Convert Format to JPEG, Compress Image at a lower quality, Add Watermark, Base64 Converter round-trip, Image to PDF export, and a messaging-app send to yourself.
  4. Read all seven outputs with the EXIF viewer and fill in your own matrix.
  5. Note the software versions. Encoder behaviour changes between releases, which is exactly why a method outlasts a table.

Appendix Q — Where the fields live in the file

"Metadata" is not one block. In a JPEG, EXIF sits in an APP1 marker whose payload begins with Exif\0\0 and contains an IFD of tagged values; XMP lives in a separate APP1 with its own namespace URI; comments live in COM markers; an ICC profile is split across APP2 markers; and an EXIF thumbnail is itself a small JPEG embedded inside the EXIF IFD. A PNG carries the same ideas in ancillary chunks such as eXIf, iCCP, tEXt and iTXt.

That structure explains the matrix. An encoder that copies APP markers keeps everything (browser save). An encoder that rebuilds the file from decoded pixels keeps nothing except what it deliberately re-writes (canvas watermark). An encoder that retains only the Orientation tag does so because dropping it without rotating the pixels would display the image sideways. And a Base64 round-trip keeps every marker because it never parses the container at all.

The embedded thumbnail deserves its own warning again: it is a *separate JPEG inside the file*, so a tool that reads only the main scan can report a clean, redacted image while the preview still shows the original. Cleaning means removing the whole EXIF IFD, not editing one GPS tag.

Appendix R — Output sizes per round

The tagged specimen is 47,954 bytes; the untagged PNG control is 13,287 bytes; the soft-alpha specimen is 26,214 bytes. A rough size change is a useful first signal that a re-encode happened, but it is not proof: a byte copy has exactly the same size, and a Base64 round-trip also has exactly the same size. Only reading the field list tells you which of those two occurred.

OperationExpected size changeWhat the size alone tells you

Size is a hint, not a verdict. The EXIF viewer is the instrument.

Appendix S — A publishing SOP you can paste into a checklist

  1. Pick the final crop and do all redaction first.
  2. Open EXIF Viewer & Cleaner, read the field list, and write it down.
  3. Strip all metadata, then re-encode once with Convert Format.
  4. Re-open the output in the viewer. The field list must be empty; if any field remains, repeat step 3.
  5. Check the pixels again — stripping metadata must not change them.
  6. If the file will pass through a chat app or CDN, send a copy to yourself and read that copy too.
  7. Record the date, tool versions and the final file size in the release note.

Step 4 is the one people skip. Without it, "we stripped the metadata" is an intention rather than a result. Step 6 catches the case where an external pipeline re-adds its own tags or re-encodes the file after your careful cleaning.

Appendix T — What we did not claim

We did not claim that every chat app behaves identically, that every encoder drops the same fields, or that a size reduction proves metadata removal. Encoder behaviour changes between versions, which is why the matrix ships with a method for reproducing it and a dated maintenance record. We also did not test proprietary "save for web" presets; their output depends on the installed version and would make this report stale within one release. Those exclusions are deliberate: a report that states its boundaries can be trusted further than one that claims universality.

Frequently asked questions

Does converting a PNG to JPG remove EXIF?

A true re-encode usually drops EXIF, XMP and comments, though Orientation is often preserved because viewers need it. A byte copy under a new name does not.

Does Base64 encoding strip metadata?

No — it returns the same bytes. If metadata is missing, a canvas render or re-encode happened before or after the Base64 step.

Can a blurred photo still leak the original through EXIF?

Yes, if the file retains an embedded EXIF thumbnail generated before the blur. Strip all metadata after editing and verify the output is empty.

Why did the colours change slightly after compression?

The ICC profile may have been dropped. Compare against a known grey patch and keep the file in one colour-managed pass.

Is a chat app a safe way to remove metadata?

It usually strips most fields, but it is not a controlled operation and it also re-encodes pixels. Strip deliberately with a viewer first, then send.
