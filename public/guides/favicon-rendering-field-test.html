Most favicon articles stop at the moment you press Export. That is the easy half. The hard half starts when the same file is asked to represent your site in a browser tab, a bookmark bar, a Windows taskbar, an iOS home screen, a PWA install prompt and a file manager — fourteen different hosts, each with its own size, background, cropping and colour rules. A mark that looks clean at 512 px can turn into an unreadable smudge at 16 px, vanish entirely against a dark tab, or lose its corners to a system mask.

This guide is a field test, not a format explanation. We took a single specimen, exported it with our own ICO & Favicon Generator, and looked at what each host actually does with it. Every screenshot below comes from that tool running in a browser; every comparison figure was drawn for this article from the same specimen. You can download both the specimen and the generated .ico at the end and repeat the whole test yourself.

What we tested, and how

The specimen is a 512 × 512 PNG with a solid rounded square, a simplified letterform and one small accent dot. It is deliberately high contrast and deliberately simple, because that is the only way to tell whether a failure comes from the artwork or from the host.

We exported a real multi-size ICO containing 16, 32, 48, 64, 128 and 256 px entries, plus single-size PNGs at each step, plus the ready-to-paste <link> tags the tool emits. Then we placed those files into a local test page and observed fourteen rendering contexts. No server-side image processing was involved, and no image was uploaded anywhere: the whole export happens in the browser.

A note on method: we are not claiming every browser on every operating system behaves identically. We are documenting the failure modes that are visible to a person looking at a screen, and tying each one back to a concrete decision you can make in the export dialog. That mapping is the useful part.

The 16 × 16 budget is smaller than it sounds

A 16 × 16 icon has exactly 256 pixels. A 512 × 512 source has 262,144. Downscaling is not "making it smaller"; it is throwing away 99.9% of the information and asking a resampling filter to invent a summary. Nothing can restore detail after that. If your logo contains a QR code, a face, a line of text, a 1-pixel stroke or a subtle gradient, those features do not become "a bit soft" — they become a few blocks of colour with no recoverable structure.

This is why our generator shows a warning next to the 16 px entry. It is not a scolding about design taste; it is arithmetic. At 16 px you get roughly a single letter, a flat geometric mark, or a two-colour high-contrast silhouette. The accent dot on our specimen survives at 16 px because it is a filled circle about 4 px across after scaling, not because the exporter is clever.

The practical consequence: draw the 16 and 32 px versions separately instead of shrinking a large image. Keep the subject filling the square, keep the silhouette simple, and raise the contrast. That is also why the six-size ICO is worth exporting rather than a single 32 px PNG: the host gets to pick an entry that was authored at (or near) the size it needs.

Dark mode is a contrast problem, not a second file

A frequent report is "my favicon disappeared when I switched to dark mode". Almost always the icon is a dark glyph on a transparent background. On a light tab it reads; on a near-black tab it does not. The fix is not to ship a second icon and hope the browser picks it — most hosts will not. The fix is to give the mark its own backing: a filled rounded square with enough luminance difference from both light and dark chrome. Our specimen uses an indigo square for exactly that reason, and it is the single change that makes cells 1, 3 and 14 all pass.

If you must keep a transparent glyph, test it against #ffffff, #f1f3f4, #202124 and #000000 before shipping. Those four swatches cover the majority of tab backgrounds. A mark that passes only two of them will generate support mail.

iOS masks and Android safe zones are cropping operations

The iOS home screen does not display your apple-touch-icon as a square. It applies a rounded mask, and it may apply a gloss or a tint depending on the OS version. Anything you place in the outer corners of the artwork is simply not shown. The same is true, more aggressively, for Android adaptive icons: the foreground layer is 108 × 108 dp but the visible mask is typically a circle whose diameter is about 66 dp, so content beyond roughly the central 80% is at risk of being clipped by the device manufacturer's chosen shape.

The rule we use: keep all meaningful content inside a centred circle about two-thirds of the canvas, and let the background colour bleed to the edges. On the specimen, the letterform sits inside that circle; the accent dot sits just outside it, which is why cell 10 in the figure shows the dot clipped. That is an intentional, visible demonstration — not a bug in the export.

There is a separate trap here. apple-touch-icon and the manifest icon set are different files with different purposes. Adding a 180 px PNG to your ICO does nothing for iOS; the ICO is a Windows/browser container. Conversely, a beautiful apple-touch-icon does nothing for a Windows taskbar. Keep them as separate deliverables with separate link tags.

Safari pinned tabs do not use the ICO at all

Pinned tabs in Safari are driven by mask-icon, a monochrome SVG whose colour is set with a color attribute. If you only ship an ICO, Safari falls back to a generic glyph or a screenshot-like letter. This is the one placement in our fourteen where exporting another bitmap size cannot help. It is worth calling out because people otherwise keep adding sizes to the ICO trying to fix a problem the ICO is not involved in.

Why the 256 entry still earns its place

It is tempting to export only 16 and 32 px: browsers mostly ask for those. Then someone opens the file in Explorer at a large icon size, or hovers a taskbar preview, or checks the file properties, and gets a blurry upscale of the 48 px entry. The 256 entry costs a few kilobytes because ICO entries can hold PNG-compressed data with an alpha channel, and it removes an entire class of "why does our logo look cheap on Windows" complaints. Since Windows Vista, an ICO directory entry may contain a complete PNG rather than a BMP plus a mask, which is why six entries fit in a small file.

Our downloadable sample contains all six. Open it in a viewer that can list ICO directory entries and you will see the six ICONDIR records; that is the concrete difference between a real multi-size ICO and a renamed PNG.

The false alarms we ruled out during the test

  • "The favicon changed in the link preview." Chat apps and social cards usually read og:image, not favicon.ico. Changing the ICO will not change that thumbnail.
  • "The icon updated on desktop but not on the phone." Mobile home-screen icons are cached at install time. Re-adding the shortcut, not re-exporting, is the fix.
  • "The icon is fine in the tab but wrong in bookmarks." Same bitmap, different background. This is a contrast issue, not a size issue.
  • "Adding more sizes made it blurrier." Only if the host picked a poorly authored entry. A 16 px entry drawn at 16 px beats a 512 px master downscaled by the browser.
  • "The ICO is transparent, so it should work everywhere." Alpha survives in PNG-compressed ICO entries, but hosts that flatten onto a solid field will not honour it.

Reproduce the test in five steps

  1. Open the ICO & Favicon Generator and load a square, high-contrast mark. Download the specimen PNG if you want the exact same input.
  2. Tick all six sizes — 16, 32, 48, 64, 128, 256 — and generate. Keep both the .ico and the individual PNGs.
  3. Paste the generated <link rel="icon"> tags into a local HTML file, add an apple-touch-icon and a minimal manifest, and serve it over http://localhost so the browser treats it as a real origin.
  4. Check the fourteen placements: tab, bookmark, dark tab, a 2× display, taskbar, three Explorer views, an iOS add-to-home-screen, an adaptive-icon preview, Safari pinned tab, PWA install card, a link preview and a high-contrast toggle.
  5. For each failure, use the table above to identify the missing entry or the wrong file type, then re-export only what changed.

Appendix A — What the six entries actually cost

A multi-size ICO is not six copies of the same PNG. Each entry is a separate image stored at its own offset in one container, and PNG compression makes the small entries cheap. Here is the directory of the exact six-size sample shipped with this pack, read straight from its ICONDIR:

EntryBit depthBytes in resourceOffset
16 × 1632625102
32 × 32321,236727
48 × 48321,8331,963
64 × 64322,4533,796
128 × 128324,6926,249
256 × 256328,88110,941

The whole file is 19,822 bytes. The 16 × 16 entry is a few hundred bytes; the 256 × 256 entry dominates. That is the honest answer to "is including six sizes wasteful": on a logo with large flat areas, the extra entries cost less than a single compressed screenshot. Since Windows Vista an ICO entry may hold a complete PNG rather than a BMP plus a 1-bit mask, which is why the container stays small and keeps a real alpha channel at every size.

Compare that with the single-size assets: the specimen PNG is 3,859 bytes and the tone chart PNG is 13,287 bytes. A flat geometric mark compresses extremely well; a photographic gradient does not. If your 256 entry is unexpectedly large, the usual cause is a photographic source rather than a drawn mark — which is itself a hint that the artwork is too detailed for a 16 px tab.

Appendix C — When an ICO is the wrong deliverable

The ICO container is a Windows/browser artefact. There are four cases where it is the wrong thing to produce:

  • iOS home screen. It needs apple-touch-icon, a square PNG with no alpha in the corners, at 180 × 180. A rounded mask is applied by the OS, so drawing your own rounded corners into the artwork produces a double-rounded shape.
  • Android adaptive icon. It needs a foreground and background layer declared in the manifest, with content inside the safe zone. A single flat ICO cannot express two layers.
  • Safari pinned tab. It needs a monochrome SVG via mask-icon. No bitmap entry of any size is consulted.
  • Social / chat link previews. Those read og:image or a platform card image. Favicon changes do not propagate there.

Shipping the ICO *and* the three other deliverables is the complete answer. Shipping a bigger ICO is not.

Appendix D — What we could not measure

We deliberately left three things out rather than guess. We did not measure every OEM's adaptive mask shape, because manufacturers change it and a claim about "the" Android mask would be wrong within a release. We did not measure favicon behaviour inside every in-app browser, because those webviews frequently cache icons at the application level rather than the site level. And we did not claim a universal cache duration, because it varies by host and by whether the URL changed. Each of those is a good candidate for a dated follow-up round; the changelog is where such an update would be recorded.

Frequently asked questions

Why is my favicon blurry at 16×16 even though the source is sharp?

Because a 16 × 16 bitmap has 256 pixels total and no resampling filter can preserve thin lines, small text or gradients at that budget. Author a separate 16 px version with a bold silhouette instead of shrinking a large master.

Do I really need a 256 px entry in favicon.ico?

Not for browser tabs, but Windows Explorer large-icon views, taskbar previews and file properties can request it. PNG-compressed ICO entries make the cost small, so including it removes a whole class of blurry-upscale reports.

Why does my icon look different on iPhone than in Chrome?

iOS applies a rounded mask to apple-touch-icon and caches home-screen icons at install time. It is a different file with different cropping, not the ICO entry.

Will adding more sizes to the ICO fix a Safari pinned tab?

No. Pinned tabs use a monochrome SVG through mask-icon. No bitmap size affects that placement.

Does the link preview in chat apps use the favicon?

Usually no — it reads og:image or a platform card image. Changing the ICO will not change that thumbnail.
